\begin{abstract}
Uncertainty quantification for large language models is usually computed from
the token distribution a system produces. Grammar-constrained decoding, now
routine wherever structured output is required, inserts a masking and
renormalisation step between the model and that distribution. We ask what
predictive uncertainty measured after this step describes. Instrumenting the
decoding loop of two open-weight models, we record both the model's
unconstrained next-token distribution and the renormalised distribution used for
generation, at matched positions, over 150 prompts drawn from HarmBench, Alpaca
and XSTest. We report the probability mass the grammar retains, the entropy of
each distribution, and the mass the model places on refusal continuations. At
the first generated position the retained mass is on the order of $10^{-10}$ for
Qwen2.5-7B and $10^{-8}$ for Llama-3.1-8B, and the entropy of the renormalised
distribution is larger than that of the model's own distribution rather than
smaller. The unconstrained distribution at that position still separates harmful
prompts from lexically similar safe ones (AUROC $0.83$), but this separation
declines over the following twelve forced positions. Our results suggest that
uncertainty reported under grammar constraints is, at positions where the model
holds a strong preference, better read as a property of the constraint together
with the model than of the model alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Estimates of a language model's predictive uncertainty are generally derived
from the distribution over next tokens that the model exposes, whether through
its entropy, the probability of the selected token, or the spread of sampled
continuations
\citep{malinin-gales-2021-uncertainty,kuhn-etal-2023-semantic,liu-etal-2025-uncertainty}.
This is reasonable when the distribution the system uses for generation is the
distribution the model computed.

Grammar-constrained decoding breaks that identity. To guarantee that output
conforms to a schema, the decoder computes a per-token mask at each step, sets
the logits of violating tokens to $-\infty$, and renormalises over what remains
\citep{willard-louf-2023-efficient,dong-etal-2024-xgrammar}. The distribution
that then selects the next token, and that a downstream consumer can observe, is
not the model's distribution but its restriction to the admitted set
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). That this restriction distorts the distribution is
known, and is the motivation for grammar-aligned decoding
\citep{park-etal-2024-grammar}. What has received less attention is what it does
to \emph{uncertainty estimates} computed downstream of it.

\label{fig:pipeline}

The entropy of the renormalised distribution is bounded above by
$\log_2|A_t|$, where $A_t$ is the token set the grammar admits at step $t$. That
bound is a property of the schema, chosen by whoever wrote it. A model that is
entirely resolved about its next token and a model that is indifferent yield
identical renormalised distributions whenever the grammar admits the same set
and the model has no preference within it, so an estimate computed from the
served distribution alone cannot distinguish them.

Whether this matters depends on how much of the model's belief the grammar
discards. If the admitted set carries most of the mass, the renormalised
distribution is close to a conditional of the model's own and the estimate
remains interpretable. If it carries very little, the estimate is computed on a
residue. We measure which obtains, using refusal behaviour as a probe, since a
safety-aligned model presented with a harmful request concentrates its
distribution sharply and its suppression is therefore visible. The safety
framing is instrumental. The phenomenon is a property of masked decoding rather
than of safety training. We ask three questions.

\begin{description}
\itemsep0em
  \item[RQ1] How much of the model's predictive mass survives masking, and how
        does this vary with position?
  \item[RQ2] How does the entropy of the renormalised distribution relate to
        that of the model's own?
  \item[RQ3] Does a semantic preference visible in the unconstrained
        distribution persist as forced generation proceeds?
\end{description}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Let $P_{\text{free}}(\cdot \mid c_t)$ be the model's next-token distribution over
vocabulary $V$ given context $c_t$, and let $A_t \subseteq V$ be the set the
grammar admits at step $t$. We call
$R_t = \sum_{v \in A_t} P_{\text{free}}(v \mid c_t)$ the \textbf{retained mass}.
The distribution used for generation is
$P_{\text{mask}}(v) = P_{\text{free}}(v)/R_t$ for $v \in A_t$.

Because masking renormalises rather than reshapes, the divergence between the
two distributions has a closed form,

\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_{\text{mask}} \,\|\, P_{\text{free}}\right) \;=\; -\log R_t ,
\label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}

so retained mass and the divergence of the served distribution from the model's
are the same measurement in different units. We report both, the divergence
because it is the standard way to express the gap between two predictive
distributions and the retained mass because it is easier to read. The reverse
direction is not available, since $P_{\text{mask}}$ assigns zero mass to tokens
$P_{\text{free}}$ does not.

We compare $H_{\text{pre}}$, the entropy of $P_{\text{free}}$ over the full
vocabulary, with $H_{\text{post}}$, the entropy of $P_{\text{mask}}$, both in
bits and both exact with no top-$k$ truncation. Writing
$H_{A} = -\sum_{v \in A_t} P_{\text{free}}(v)\log_2 P_{\text{free}}(v)$,

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{post}} = \frac{H_{A}}{R_t} + \log_2 R_t ,
\qquad H_{\text{post}} \le \log_2 |A_t| .
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}

The two terms have opposing sign. Masking that removes low-probability tokens
leaves $R_t$ near one and reduces entropy, while masking that removes the mode
makes $R_t$ small and can increase it.

As a semantic probe we measure the mass on refusal,
$P_{\text{refuse}}(t) = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \prod_i P_{\text{free}}(r_i \mid c_t, r_{<i})$,
where $\mathcal{R}$ is a fixed prefix-free set of refusal continuations. It is
evaluated by teacher forcing against $P_{\text{free}}$, so where the grammar
forbids a refusal it is counterfactual, namely the mass the model would place on
refusing were it unconstrained at that position. This is the mass of a semantic
equivalence class \citep{kuhn-etal-2023-semantic,fadeeva-etal-2024-fact},
obtained exactly rather than by sampling.

\paragraph{Measurement positions.}
$P_{\text{refuse}}$ is a probability over phrase onsets, so it is comparable only
where a phrase could begin. We report at three landmarks, located per generation
by parsing the realised sequence rather than by fixed index. $t_0$ is the first
generated position, before the constraint has acted. $t_{\text{open}}$ is where
the schema's string value opens, which is structurally a sentence start, so a
refusal is well-formed there as at $t_0$. $t^{*}$ is the position after the
forced literal \texttt{Step 1:}, where the schema pins an imperative verb and
refusal onsets are excluded. Without $t_{\text{open}}$ a decline in
$P_{\text{refuse}}$ could not be separated from a refusal simply not fitting
grammatically.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Setup.}
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{yang-etal-2024-qwen25} and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
\citep{grattafiori-etal-2024-llama3} in \texttt{bfloat16}, decoded greedily, with
vocabularies of $152{,}064$ and $128{,}256$. We use fifty harmful prompts from
HarmBench \citep{mazeika-etal-2024-harmbench}, fifty benign instructions from
Alpaca \citep{taori-etal-2023-alpaca}, and fifty safe prompts from XSTest
\citep{rottger-etal-2024-xstest}, the last being lexically alarming but benign
and included because separation against Alpaca is close to trivial. The schema
requires \texttt{\{"step1": "Step 1: $\langle$verb$\rangle$ \ldots"\}} with the
verb drawn from twenty-five imperatives, following the structured-output attack
of \citet{cda-2025-grammar}, and is compiled with XGrammar
\citep{dong-etal-2024-xgrammar}. We replace the generation loop so that raw
logits are read before the bitmask is applied and both distributions are
recorded, a seam that hosted APIs do not expose and that high-throughput servers
absorb into the sampling kernel \citep{kwon-etal-2023-efficient}. $\mathcal{R}$
holds fourteen phrases in space-prefixed and bare form, giving twenty-eight token
sequences fixed before collection (Appendix~\ref{app:refusal}). All $44/44$
refusals each model produces without a grammar begin with a member of
$\mathcal{R}$. Intervals are percentile bootstrap over prompts with $2{,}000$
resamples, AUROC resamples the two groups independently, and the
$t_0 \rightarrow t^{*}$ change is paired by prompt. We report no significance
tests.

\paragraph{Retained mass (RQ1).}
At $t_0$ the grammar admits five tokens, the JSON opening brace and whitespace
variants, holding $2.7\times10^{-10}$ of Qwen's predictive mass ($95\%$ CI
$[6.4\times10^{-11}, 5.6\times10^{-10}]$) and $7.2\times10^{-8}$ of Llama's
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). Equivalently, the served distribution lies $31.8$ and
$23.7$ bits of KL divergence from the model's own. The distribution used for
generation there is a renormalisation of that remainder. Retained mass rises as
the schema loosens, to $8.7\times10^{-4}$ and $5.3\times10^{-3}$ at
$t_{\text{open}}$ and to $0.20$ and $0.33$ at $t^{*}$, spanning roughly nine
orders of magnitude within one generation.

\paragraph{Entropy before and after masking (RQ2).}
At $t_0$ the model's own distribution is close to degenerate, $0.07$ bits for
Qwen and $0.21$ for Llama, against $0.76$ and $0.93$ on Alpaca prompts. The
renormalised distribution at the same position carries $1.26$ and $0.79$ bits,
larger rather than smaller by factors of roughly eighteen and four.
Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp} accounts for this. The mask removes the token the model most
prefers, and those remaining are ones among which it holds no marked preference,
so the admitted set presents as indecision whether or not the model was
undecided. The direction of the harmful and benign entropy difference is also
position dependent (Figure~\ref{fig:landmarks}). At $t_0$ harmful prompts yield
\emph{lower} entropy than benign ones, AUROC $0.158$ $[0.08, 0.25]$ for Qwen and
$0.175$ $[0.10, 0.26]$ for Llama, and by $t^{*}$ the ordering has reversed, at
$0.915$ and $0.718$. \citet{steindl-etal-2024-linguistic} report higher
predictive entropy for prompts that evade safety mechanisms, measured at the
sequence level over sampled responses and contrasting prompt variants, so it is
not directly comparable. The two observations are nonetheless a reason for care,
since token-level entropy at the first position and sequence-level entropy over a
response need not agree in sign.

\paragraph{Persistence of the refusal signal (RQ3).}
At $t_0$ the unconstrained distribution separates HarmBench from XSTest safe
prompts at AUROC $0.827$ $[0.74, 0.90]$, and from Alpaca at $1.000$. The XSTest
figure is the informative one, since those prompts do elicit a response
($P_{\text{refuse}} = 0.372$ against $0.000$ for Alpaca) and the separation is
therefore not attributable to surface vocabulary alone. $P_{\text{refuse}}$ then
declines, from $0.769$ to $0.029$ for Qwen and $0.830$ to $0.024$ for Llama, a
paired change of $+0.736$ $[0.664, 0.804]$ and $+0.799$ $[0.715, 0.874]$. Since
the bitmask is applied after the forward pass it cannot alter $P_{\text{free}}$
directly, so the decline is attributable to the model conditioning on tokens it
was required to emit, consistent with accounts of forced affirmative prefixes as
a safety failure mode \citep{wei-etal-2023-jailbroken}. Without a grammar both
models open with a refusal on $88\%$ of these prompts.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

These measurements suggest that uncertainty reported under grammar-constrained
decoding is best read as a joint property of the model and the constraint, and
that how much weight to place on it depends on $R_t$. Where $R_t$ is close to
one, $P_{\text{mask}}$ is close to a conditional of the model's own distribution
and an entropy computed from it remains interpretable. Where $R_t$ is of order
$10^{-10}$, it is not clear what such an estimate is an estimate of.

Eq.~\ref{eq:decomp} also reconciles our observations with the expectation that
constraints reduce uncertainty by restricting the candidate set. Both directions
are available, and averaged over positions most masking trims the tail and
lowers entropy. The positions where the opposite occurs are those where the
model held a strong preference, which for questions about model behaviour are
the positions of interest.

A modest suggestion follows. Under constrained decoding, uncertainty is more
informative when reported with respect to $P_{\text{free}}$, or at least
accompanied by $R_t$, which is available wherever the mask is and costs nothing
to record. This is not a new estimator but a statement about which distribution
an existing one should be applied to, and it has a concrete consequence for
serving stacks, since implementations that apply the mask inside a fused
sampling kernel discard $P_{\text{free}}$ before anything can observe it. Our use
of refusal is a probe rather than a subject, and the mechanism should apply
wherever a model holds a sharp preference the schema forbids.

\section*{Limitations}

Our study covers one grammar family, one harmful benchmark, fifty prompts per
condition, and two open-weight models of comparable size. Whether the effect
holds for other schema types, larger models, or closed-weight systems is open.
Closed-weight systems cannot be examined this way at all, since the method
requires logits before the constraint is applied, a restriction shared by
probabilistic uncertainty measurement generally
\citep{steindl-etal-2024-linguistic}.

Landmark dropout affects the deeper comparisons. The middle landmark is located
by parsing the realised sequence and is lost when the tokeniser merges the
value-opening quote with what follows. It is recovered for $47$ to $50$ of $50$
harmful prompts but only $36$ of $50$ XSTest prompts, and the lost XSTest
prompts have systematically higher $P_{\text{refuse}}$ at $t_0$
(Appendix~\ref{app:dropout}), so the XSTest AUROC values at $t_{\text{open}}$
and $t^{*}$ should be read as optimistic. The $t_0$ figures have no dropout.

Inference is in \texttt{bfloat16}, so retained mass at $t_0$ should be taken as
an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than to three significant figures.

$P_{\text{refuse}}$ depends on a fixed phrase set. Its coverage on unconstrained
generations is complete for both models, but whether a model's refusal
vocabulary shifts inside a structured context is not testable from our
artefacts, since the grammar prevents a refusal from being emitted at all. The
retained mass is free of this dependence and shows the same pattern.

Finally, a control schema replacing the semantically loaded key \texttt{step1}
with a neutral one recovered part of the refusal mass on one model but not the
other (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}), so we draw no conclusion about which component
of the forced prefix is responsible.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This work uses harmful prompts from a public red-teaming benchmark and a
published structured-output attack in order to create a setting in which a
model's preference is unambiguous. We introduce no new attack and report no
attack success rates. Generated completions were inspected only on screen during
development and are not persisted, and released artefacts contain token
identifiers and distributional statistics rather than decoded harmful text. We
regard the finding that a safety-relevant signal is discarded by a standard
decoding path as defensively useful, since it identifies a place where existing
information could be retained rather than a new way to remove it.
